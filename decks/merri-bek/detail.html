Twenty gardens.
A record that keeps growing.

Ecological Registry builds a continuing ecological record for private land. We begin with assessed gardens in Melbourne, recording their condition and changes over time. We’re developing tools that help councils and practitioners collect evidence, track outcomes and carry that history through redevelopment and property handover.

Each participating garden would hold a dated ecological baseline, a clear account of its evidence and a way to record what happens next. Together, those records could give Council a more useful picture of private-land ecology.

The record holds what is growing, the garden’s structure and habitat, its relationship to surrounding land and the evidence of change. It sits alongside Council’s existing programmes, including Gardens for Wildlife; it is not a certification scheme or a regulation.

Properties accumulate records of their buildings, energy and water. Their living systems rarely have a record that survives a change of owner. The Registry’s purpose is to carry that ecological history forward, while keeping personal information separate and making the limits of the evidence visible.

01 · Proposed pilot

A small cohort, shaped together.

Scope: 20 voluntarily participating gardens. This is the proposal discussed, not an approved or recruited cohort. Participant criteria, responsibilities, timing and costs remain to be agreed with Council.

  1. Council invites. Merri-bek communicates the opportunity through its own channels. Its resident list stays with Council.
  2. The steward opts in. A resident approaches the Registry directly, provides their own information and chooses what may be published. Existing Gardens for Wildlife support remains available without Registry participation.
  3. A baseline is recorded. A steward may provide their own Gardens for Wildlife assessment. We identify what it supports, what is missing and what needs further assessment. A Council report is evidence, not Council verification of a Registry score.
  4. The garden develops. The record helps the steward identify practical next steps for planting, soil, water and habitat.
  5. We return to the evidence. A follow-up around 12 months is proposed, subject to agreement. We record establishment and losses as well as improvements, and state where follow-up evidence is missing.

The pilot should test usefulness to residents and officers, assessment effort, data completeness and repeatability. It cannot establish mature habitat recovery or biodiversity net gain from a score increase alone.

02 · Steward participation

Make the assessment useful beyond the visit.

A visit creates a moment of attention. A continuing record gives the steward a baseline to return to, practical next steps and a way to see what has changed. The pilot would test whether this helps people continue caring for their garden after the initial assessment.

Research informing this approach includes Mumaw and Bekessy’s study of wildlife gardening and collaborative public–private biodiversity conservation (2017). The pilot’s own engagement and ecological outcomes would still need to be measured.

Observe

Establish a baseline from an assessment, a programme report the steward provides or a self-assessment. Keep the source and verification status visible.

Improve

Show ranked next steps, with practical do-it-yourself actions where appropriate. Recommendations calculate the possible score change using the same scoring engine.

Update

The steward reports a new habitat feature, planting or observation. A submitted claim remains a claim until its underlying evidence has been reviewed.

Recognise

Confirmed inputs determine scores, tiers and badges. Read progress against the garden’s own history, alongside its evidence, rather than as a household competition.

What the steward can do.

  • Access their record. The existing steward pathway uses an emailed single-use link rather than a password, with access to personal details and privacy controls.
  • See specific next steps. Profile recommendations show the potential points from an eligible change. A shallow water source, for example, can add three habitat points where that feature is absent; it is not a universal bonus.
  • Report a completed step. “I’ve done this” submits a claim for review. It does not award points. Confirmation changes the underlying input, after which the score is recalculated.
  • Record observations. Field notes and species additions support the continuing record; they do not automatically change an assessed score. The proposed distinction between steward additions and confirmed evidence needs to remain explicit as this workflow develops.
  • Receive recognition. Badges recognise recorded attributes and the six tiers describe the current score. Neither a badge nor a tier replaces the record’s verification status.

The inputs are the truth. The score is a consequence. A click is not evidence that a habitat feature exists. Review adds a step, but protects an assessed record from silently absorbing an unverified claim.

What remains in development.

Property handover. The intended handover pack would carry ecological history to a new steward without transferring the previous steward’s personal information. The handover and access-revocation workflow remains development work, not a completed pilot service.

Yield. A future funding layer is being designed so that maintaining genuine habitat could attract support. It is not active and pays no one today. No payment or funding timeline is promised through this pilot.

03 · Current scoring

What the score means today.

The Registry’s published method scores a garden out of 100. It combines ecological indicators with the strength of its documentation. It is a practical garden assessment, with limitations that remain visible.

PillarMaximumWhat is recorded
Biodiversity structure25Indigenous species, indigenous dominance, vegetation layers and canopy cover.
Soil & water function20Soil health, water function, rainwater capture, moisture basins and swales.
Habitat complexity20Habitat nodes, logs, rocks, water features, nest boxes and fauna evidence.
Connectivity20Proximity to habitat, neighbouring registered gardens, corridor role and contiguous cluster area.
Evidence & verification15Photographs, field notes, species and fauna records, professional assessment and verification level.

Six tiers: Foundation Garden (0–20), Habitat Garden (21–40), Ecological Garden (41–60), Rich Habitat Garden (61–80), Ecosystem Garden (81–90) and Urban Biodiversity Node (91–100).

Absolute species thresholds and the cluster-area component can favour larger properties. Comparing Merri-bek’s small courtyards and larger gardens fairly requires further work on available space and ecological opportunity. Those proposed changes do not alter today’s scores.

Fixed species, canopy and vegetation-layer thresholds do not yet adjust to the reference community for a site. Documentation can also raise the score without changing the garden. Verification status therefore needs to be read alongside the number.

04 · Accounting for Nature

An external reference for the method’s development.

The Registry is not AfN-certified. A Registry score is not an Econd®. Accounting for Nature’s Econd measures environmental condition against a defined reference benchmark using an accredited method. Sharing a 0–100 scale does not make the scores equivalent.

AfN informs the direction of our work: appropriate ecological reference benchmarks, clear evidence and formal assurance. Identifying a site’s pre-clearing vegetation community provides context; calibrating indicators against that community is a further step that remains to be completed.

We also need to separate ecological condition from confidence in the evidence and establish independent repeatability. AfN offers certification through an independent audit or an AfN technical assessment; G&S verification is neither of these.

Future opportunity-relative scoring would ask how well a garden uses its available space. That is a different measure from condition against an ecological reference and would remain clearly labelled as such.

Sources: AfN’s explanation of Econd and verification and audit pathways.

05 · Council data

A record that can reach the tools Council already uses.

The Registry adds a consistent account of what happens after planting, across properties and years. It can support Council’s existing advice, species lists and reporting systems. The fields, geographic grouping and delivery format for a Merri-bek pilot would be agreed with officers.

A data feed

The public register and individual garden records are published as JSON over HTTPS. Council rollups already provide a pattern for reporting; a Merri-bek feed would be generated from participating Merri-bek records once the cohort and publication rules are agreed.

The demonstration dashboard is not a live feed of measured Merri-bek outcomes. Its example figures are separate from the wider-register figures above and the proposed 20-garden cohort.

For reporting, the existing rollup pattern supplies a numerator and denominator (k qualifying records out of n eligible records), the eligibility definition, an as_at date, methodology version and source commit. Summaries are derived from the records at build time so that a reported result can be traced to its source. Missing evidence must remain visible.

Measured observations, derived calculations and modelled projections need distinct labels. A change in documentation must not be presented as a change in ecological condition. We can agree the fields, reporting geography and export format that fit Council’s systems.

A Registry ID can link a feed row to a public profile. Such data is pseudonymous, not anonymous. Removing names and addresses alone does not establish that a household cannot be identified. Publication and aggregation need to be reviewed together.

06 · Participation & governance

Council keeps its resident information.

Council would send the invitation itself. No participant list is transferred, and no household record is created in anticipation of consent. Any aggregate information supplied separately by Council must remain permanently de-identified and cannot be linked back to a household.

Gardener & Son operates the Registry and is also a design studio. No design services will be marketed or solicited to residents joining through this pilot. Requests for design help during the pilot will be referred elsewhere. Steward-facing materials carry Registry branding, and participant information is used solely for Registry purposes.

Public location and personal details. The intended council pathway publishes a locality and Registry identifier, with personal details and precise location held separately. Offset map pins can reduce location precision; they are not a guarantee of anonymity. Consent and location handling must be checked across profiles, maps and downloadable data, not just the visible page.

Evidence is not endorsement. A resident supplying a Council assessment does not make Council the Registry assessor or verifier. Source, assessment and verification responsibilities remain separately identified.

Publication settings, location handling, information security and retention need to be reviewed through Council’s own processes before recruitment. The dashboard uses Merri-bek geography and fictional demonstration garden locations; it does not map pilot participants. It does not establish that all existing Registry data surfaces meet the requirements of a council pilot.

Council Partnership Principles v1.0 apply to this proposal. We welcome Council’s probity and privacy review, including any requirements of its Gardens for Wildlife partnership.

07 · Next step

Agree what would make the pilot useful.

The next conversation can settle the cohort, assessment responsibilities, reporting needs, follow-up timing and budget. The dashboard demonstrates a Merri-bek council view with local geography and illustrative garden, reporting and scenario figures. It does not show measured Merri-bek outcomes; the proposed pilot remains 20 gardens.

  • Before invitations: agree selection, consent, data fields, responsibilities, costs, funding and reporting. Verification visits are a paid service; no unagreed work is assumed to be funded.
  • Around three months: review participation, return visits to the record, observations submitted and whether residents find the next steps useful.
  • Around twelve months: reassess ecological features and establishment, recording losses and missing evidence as well as gains. Distinguish actual garden change from better documentation.

These are proposed checkpoints to settle together, not promised results or a confirmed timetable.

We can also facilitate conversations with council contacts involved in the Registry discussions in other municipalities. Those conversations are exploratory and should not be read as Council endorsement or adoption.
