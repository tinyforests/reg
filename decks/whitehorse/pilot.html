Ecological Registry

A small Whitehorse pilot — outline for discussion

Prepared for
Leonie Gibson · Natural Environment Strategy
Sue Ann Yap · Gardens for Wildlife
August 2026

Gardens for Wildlife already does the hard part. Two Garden Guides visit a private garden, look at what is actually growing and living there, and write it up. What follows this outline is one idea: that the assessment could also become the beginning of a long-term ecological record rather than a single point in time. This is a test of that idea at a deliberately small scale — shaped around the two points raised at our first meeting.

What we propose
Scale
10–20 Whitehorse gardens, existing or upcoming Gardens for Wildlife participants, entirely voluntary.
New visits
None. The Registry does not need to create another visit — the value is in what already happens.
Evidence
A small agreed set drawn from the steward's own Gardens for Wildlife assessment, with the source and its verification status recorded rather than treated as equivalent to a field visit.
Duration
Registry baseline at joining, then one steward-provided update at twelve months.
Cost to Council
No fee, minimal additional officer time, and no change to the Gardens for Wildlife assessment process.

How it would work

Council invites

Whitehorse tells its own Gardens for Wildlife participants the pilot exists. No list changes hands.

Steward registers

The resident comes to the Registry themselves and consents in their own name.

Assessment provided

The steward supplies their own copy of their Gardens for Wildlife assessment directly to the Registry.

Baseline recorded

Biodiversity structure, soil and water, habitat complexity, connectivity, evidence.

Twelve months on

The steward updates the record. The pilot provides an aggregated picture of whether participating gardens are developing.

Privacy — no resident data transfer

For this pilot, Whitehorse does not provide resident information to the Registry. Council simply lets Gardens for Wildlife participants know the pilot exists. A steward who wishes to participate approaches the Registry themselves, consents directly, and supplies their own assessment. Records are created only by the steward — the Registry never receives Whitehorse's participant list and never pre-builds a household record.

Whitehorse remains the custodian of its resident information. Consent comes from the steward rather than being inferred, and Council never needs to transfer resident information to a third party. Any future municipal reporting would be aggregated and de-identified.

Separation from our design work
  • No design services marketed or solicited to any steward who joins through the Gardens for Wildlife pathway.
  • Discreet Registry branding on steward-facing pilot materials.
  • Sole purpose — information supplied directly by participating stewards is used for Registry purposes only.

The Registry already includes gardens that Gardener & Son had no role in designing or building, so that separation is already reflected in how it operates. We would also be very comfortable working within any conflict-of-interest, partnership or probity framework Council considers appropriate for the pilot.

Where we think this could be useful to Whitehorse
A clearer picture of private land

Where good gardens already are, where the habitat gaps sit, and where clusters are forming.

Connectivity beyond council land

An isolated reserve and twenty nearby gardens read as stepping stones rather than separate sites.

Evidence over time

Whether gardens are genuinely developing structure, habitat and soil function — not only how many plants were given away.

A record that persists

A long-term ecological record can carry across years and changes of ownership, without carrying personal information forward.

How a steward would take the next step

A steward who uploads their own Gardens for Wildlife assessment sees their ecological baseline, their strongest area and their clearest gap — and can then redeem their Gardens for Wildlife plant voucher with that gap in front of them. Council's plant support gets directed by evidence rather than guesswork, and at twelve months there is a record of whether it worked.

This would be the default pathway rather than the only one — Council's existing redemption process stays available on request, so no resident is required to join the Registry to receive a voucher they are already entitled to.

What we're asking

Not for Whitehorse to adopt a finished system. Only whether a group this small is worth trying together, and what you would need to see before it could be considered further. We would like to shape it with you.

Further reading — Mumaw & Bekessy, "Wildlife gardening for collaborative public–private biodiversity conservation", Australasian Journal of Environmental Management, 2017. An RMIT study of a Victorian Gardens for Wildlife program; the authors recommend a participatory monitoring component, and found members willing to record and contribute that information themselves.
