Economic activation layer — concept

The garden already does the work. YIELD is the question of who pays for it.

A registered garden holds water, carries a corridor, shelters fauna and builds soil — services a city would otherwise fund through infrastructure. YIELD asks whether the households doing that work can be paid for it, by the organisations that need the outcome. This portal sets out how that could function, and where it might not.

How it would work

Four parties, one ledger

YIELD does not create a new measurement. It attaches a payment to a measurement that already exists — the verified Registry score, produced independently of anyone buying anything. That separation is the whole design: the assessor never knows what a garden is worth to a sponsor.

01

Garden is verified

An independent site assessment produces a score across five pillars. This happens whether or not YIELD exists, and is never adjusted for value.

→
02

Function becomes units

Verified ecological function converts to units on a published, fixed conversion. Units track the garden's actual condition, and fall if it declines.

→
03

A sponsor contracts

A council, developer, water authority or corporate program contracts units against a stated obligation or commitment. Contracts are multi-year.

→
04

The steward is paid

Payment flows to the household holding the garden, on condition of continued verification. Non-verification stops payment.

Who holds the risk

Design intent

The steward is paid for condition maintained, not for a promise. If a garden's verified score falls, units fall with it — the ledger has no mechanism to pay for function that is not there.

What a sponsor buys

Design intent

A contracted claim on verified private-land ecological function, in a named locality, auditable to individual properties, with a documented methodology behind it.

What G&S takes

Undecided

A platform margin on contracted units, structured so the Registry earns from throughput rather than from any garden scoring higher. The rate is not set.

What stops this being greenwashing

Integrity constraints

Every voluntary environmental market has been damaged by the same failures: unverifiable claims, additionality that was never real, and measurement paid for by the party who benefits from a high number. YIELD is only worth building if it is designed against those failures from the start.

  • Measurement is separated from sale. The verified score is produced by an assessment process that has no visibility of what a garden is worth to any sponsor, and no figure is ever reverse-engineered to reach a target.
  • Payment is conditional on re-verification, not on a one-off assessment. Function that lapses stops earning.
  • Self-reported gardens generate no units under any circumstances. Only site-visit-verified condition enters the ledger.
  • Units are not offsets and must not be sold as a licence to damage elsewhere. If sponsors want offsets, this is the wrong instrument and they should be told so.
  • Additionality is the unresolved problem, stated plainly: paying for a garden that would have existed anyway buys nothing real. Any credible version of YIELD needs a defensible answer here, and does not yet have one.
  • The methodology is published, versioned, and open to being audited against Accounting for Nature's framework. A ledger nobody can inspect is worth nothing.
The inputs are the truth, the score is the consequence — and the price is a separate conversation that must never reach back into either.The constraint YIELD has to hold

Sequence

Current position

The honest revenue order is Steward Review first, council pilot second, YIELD at scale third. YIELD sits furthest out deliberately — it needs a verified ledger of real depth and institutional citation behind it before any payment mechanism is credible. Building it early would discredit the ledger it depends on.

Open questions

Unresolved by design

These are the questions that decide whether YIELD is a real instrument or a nice idea. They are listed here rather than hidden because a concept that only shows its strengths is not a concept, it is a pitch.

Who actually pays, and under what obligation?

Voluntary corporate spend is fragile and first to be cut. Obligation-driven demand — offset requirements, water authority targets, developer conditions — is durable but brings regulatory scrutiny this methodology has not yet faced.

Can additionality be demonstrated?

Most registered gardens were built because their owners wanted them, not because of a payment. Paying for what would have happened anyway is the failure mode that has discredited every market like this one.

Is the payment large enough to matter?

Run the estimator at any plausible rate. If a household recovers a small fraction of installation cost over five years, YIELD is recognition rather than incentive — which may still be worth building, but is a different product.

What happens when a property sells?

Contracts run for years; households move sooner. Either the obligation travels with the title, or the ledger accepts churn — both have consequences for what a sponsor is actually buying.

Who verifies at scale?

A ledger verified by the same studio that designs and installs the gardens cannot support a payment mechanism. Independent verification capacity is a precondition, not a later refinement.

Does the money change the gardens?

The moment function has a price, the incentive to optimise for the metric rather than the ecology appears. Every scoring pillar becomes a target, and targets distort. This one has no clean solution.
