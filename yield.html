THE ECONOMIC LAYER

The garden already does the work. Yield is the question of who pays for it.

A registered garden holds water, carries a corridor, shelters fauna and builds soil — services a city would otherwise fund through infrastructure. Yield asks whether the households doing that work could be paid for it, by the organisations that need the outcome.

Yield is not active. No sponsor has contracted units, no steward has received a payment, and estimated yield today is $0 for every garden on the Registry. This page describes a mechanism under design and the questions it has not yet answered. It is not an offer and no figure on it is payable.

THE HONEST SEQUENCE
  1. 01
    Steward Review

    Paid ecological assessment by G&S. The only current commercial product, and the active revenue line.

  2. 02
    Council pilot

    Paid partnership for measurement and reporting, once a council reaches credibility density.

  3. 03
    Yield at scale

    Sponsor-funded, disbursed to stewards. Activated only when councils are active and a sponsor pipeline is real.

Stages are not skipped. Yield sits third deliberately — it needs a verified ledger of real depth behind it before any payment mechanism is credible.

FOUR PARTIES, ONE LEDGER

How it would work.

Yield would not create a new measurement. It would attach a contribution to a measurement that already exists — the verified Registry score, produced independently of anyone buying anything. That separation is the whole design: the assessor never knows what a garden is worth to a sponsor.

01 · VERIFIED

A garden is verified

An independent site assessment produces a score across five pillars. This happens whether or not yield exists, and is never adjusted for value.

02 · UNITS

Function becomes units

Verified function would convert to units on a published conversion. Units would track actual condition and fall if it declined. Self-reported gardens generate nothing.

03 · CONTRACTED

A sponsor contracts

A council, water authority, developer or corporate program would contract units against a stated obligation or commitment.

04 · SPLIT

The contribution splits

A contribution would divide three ways: to the household holding the garden, to the verification that makes the unit real, and to running the ledger.

Verification is funded from the contribution itself, not by the steward and not by the sponsor of that particular garden. Allocations would pool, so nobody pays for the assessment of the garden they are buying — the cost of proof sits inside the instrument rather than depending on goodwill.

INTEGRITY CONSTRAINTS

What would stop this being greenwashing.

Every voluntary environmental market has been damaged by the same failures: unverifiable claims, additionality that was never real, and measurement paid for by the party who benefits from a high number. Yield is only worth building if it is designed against those failures from the start.

  • Measurement is separated from sale. The verified score is produced by an assessment process with no visibility of what a garden is worth to any sponsor, and no figure is ever reverse-engineered to reach a target.
  • Verification is funded from the contribution, with allocations pooled so no sponsor funds the assessment of the garden it is contracting.
  • Yield would be conditional on re-verification, not on a one-off assessment. Function that lapses would stop earning.
  • Self-reported gardens generate no units under any circumstances. Only site-visit-verified condition would enter the ledger.
  • Units are not offsets and must not be sold as a licence to damage elsewhere. If sponsors want offsets, this is the wrong instrument and they should be told so.
  • The methodology is published, versioned, and open to audit against Accounting for Nature's framework. A ledger nobody can inspect is worth nothing.

The inputs are the truth, the score is the consequence — and the price is a separate conversation that must never reach back into either.

UNRESOLVED BY DESIGN

Open questions.

These decide whether yield is a real instrument or a nice idea. They are published rather than hidden because a concept that only shows its strengths is not a concept, it is a pitch.

Who actually pays, and under what obligation?

Voluntary corporate spend is fragile and first to be cut. Obligation-driven demand — offset requirements, water authority targets, developer conditions — is durable but brings regulatory scrutiny this methodology has not yet faced.

Can additionality be demonstrated?

Most registered gardens were built because their owners wanted them, not because of a payment. Paying for what would have happened anyway is the failure mode that has discredited every market like this one.

Is the amount large enough to matter?

Run the model at any plausible rate. If a household would recover a small fraction of installation cost over five years, yield is recognition rather than incentive — which may still be worth building, but is a different product.

What happens when a property sells?

Contracts would run for years; households move sooner. Either the obligation travels with the title, or the ledger accepts churn — both have consequences for what a sponsor is actually buying.

Who verifies at scale?

A ledger verified by the same studio that designs and installs the gardens cannot support a payment mechanism. Funding verification from the contribution answers who pays for assessment; it does not answer who carries it out. Independent capacity is a precondition, not a later refinement.

Does the money change the gardens?

The moment function has a price, the incentive to optimise for the metric rather than the ecology appears. Every scoring pillar becomes a target, and targets distort. This one has no clean solution.
